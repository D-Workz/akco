Rule mining-based

Rule mining-based approaches analyze the structure of a knowledge graph to identify frequently occurring patterns—i.e., reoccurring subgraphs—and express these patterns as logic rules. These rules help verify or correct property-value assertions by generalizing observed correlations. The approach draws on ideas from grammar induction and association rule mining in databases [1].

Knowledge graphs present unique challenges for rule mining due to their large scale and the open-world assumption, which allows missing values to be interpreted as unknown. To address this, AMIE3 introduces optimizations such as pruning, parallelization, and the Partial Completeness Assumption (PCA), which assumes that if some properties are known, all relevant ones are known [2].

Figure: Deriving Logic Rules from Subgraphs

Figure 10 illustrates the core concept of rule mining. At the top, a small subgraph from the knowledge graph (originally from Figure 1) shows three entities (x, y, z) connected via locatedIn and officialCurrency relationships. This correlation is translated into a logic rule, shown in the second row:

officialCurrency(x, z) ∧ locatedIn(x, y) ⇒ officialCurrency(y, z)

This rule states that if a node x has currency z and is located in y, then y is likely to also use currency z. Multiple rules can be generated from the same subgraph. For instance:

officialCurrency(x, z) ∧ officialCurrency(y, z) ⇒ locatedIn(x, y)

The ability to derive several plausible rules from a small subgraph increases the complexity of rule mining and necessitates efficient algorithms. These rules often take the form of Horn rules, which offer a good balance between expressivity and computational scalability [3].

Evaluating Rule Validity

The row below the rule in Figure 10 shows its validity score, which is based on how often the rule holds true. This score is computed by identifying counterexamples—instances where the premise holds, but the conclusion does not. In this case, the validity is 7/9, meaning two out of nine matched subgraphs do not support the rule.

One such counterexample is shown at the bottom of the figure: sydney is connected to aud and unitedStates, but unitedStates does not use aud as currency, violating the rule. Identifying such patterns under the open-world assumption is complex, as not all missing values are true negatives. Methods like KV-rule attempt to detect these inconsistencies reliably [4].

Rule-Based Corrections

Rules are also used to suggest corrections to erroneous assertions. For example:

  • CorHist: Learns correction patterns from the edit history of a knowledge graph [5].
  • GRR: Extends AMIE3 to handle incompleteness, conflicts, and redundancies in graph structures [6].

Rule mining remains a promising area for improving both verification and correction of knowledge graphs. Future work could enhance scalability, interpretability, and better support for the open-world assumption.
