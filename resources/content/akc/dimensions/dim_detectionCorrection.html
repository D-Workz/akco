Detection- vs. correction

Knowledge cleaning consists of two distinct yet related tasks: error detection and error correction. While correction typically requires prior detection, not all detection processes are followed by correction. This section first outlines shared aspects, then highlights the specific characteristics of each task.

Approaches that support error correction usually also perform detection, but detection alone does not imply correction. Multiple techniques may exist for detecting or correcting a particular error. The primary distinction between them lies in the cleaning technique they employ and the type of background knowledge used as context. Assertions are either compared to this context to verify their correctness or searched against it to identify appropriate corrections.

Error Detection

An error detection process may receive one or more knowledge graphs or isolated assertions as input. If only assertions are provided, external background knowledge is essential; otherwise, the knowledge graph itself can serve that role. The output is a set of potentially erroneous assertions, possibly accompanied by evidence supporting their classification.

To identify syntactic errors, approaches typically rely on syntax specifications. For semantic errors, two strategies are commonly used: verification and validation.

  • Verification detects violations of formal specifications (e.g., ontologies, shape constraints) and can reveal both syntactic and semantic errors, since syntactic flaws may cause semantic inconsistencies (see Error Nature).
  • Validation checks whether an assertion semantically fits within the intended domain of discourse.

Evaluating the correctness of detection results is critical for downstream processes. Due to inherent complexity, detection methods may yield false negatives—valid assertions incorrectly flagged as erroneous— or false positives—errors flagged due to other underlying issues.

Error Correction

Error correction operates on one or more erroneous assertions, supported by background knowledge. It may follow error detection or work independently to clean errors in bulk. The core goal is to identify and apply a suitable correction based on context. If correction fails, alternatives such as manual intervention or deletion must be considered.

Like detection, correction is a non-trivial, error-prone process and should be validated before altering the knowledge graph. Corrections themselves may introduce new inconsistencies, making knowledge cleaning an inherently iterative process.

Syntactic errors are corrected by ensuring that erroneous entities conform to the syntax rules or serialization format of the domain. For semantic errors, different correction strategies are available depending on the type of violation:

  • Identifying entities supported by the formal specification
  • Modifying assertions to align with specification constraints
  • Correcting the semantic misrepresentation
  • Deleting the erroneous assertion
  • Extending the specification itself to accommodate the assertion

In cases where semantic errors arise from incorrect domain modeling (i.e., domain of discourse mismatch), the assertion must be updated to better reflect the intended real-world interpretation.
